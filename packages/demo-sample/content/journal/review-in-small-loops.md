## Share something concrete

Large reviews tend to go wrong in the same way. After weeks of quiet work, a team unveils a finished design. The room nods politely, then the real feedback arrives by email over the following fortnight, and much of it contradicts the rest.

We avoid this by reviewing small things often. A narrow draft gives collaborators a real choice to react to. Capture what changed, decide what remains open, and prepare the next useful slice.

## A worked example

A small software company asked us to rewrite the onboarding emails for its accounting tool. The first brief asked for a finished set of nine emails in a month.

Instead we sent one email in the first week. It was rough, with notes in the margin explaining each choice. The support lead replied within a day: customers never read past the second paragraph, so the key step had to come first.

That single comment reshaped all nine emails. We rewrote the first one, reviewed it again, then drafted the next two using the same pattern. Each week brought a small batch and a short call. By week four the set was done, and the support lead had shaped every one of them.

Had we delivered all nine at once, we would have repeated the same mistake nine times.

## Make each loop count

Small loops only work when each one is focused. We keep three rules for every review.

First, share one thing at a time, and say what kind of feedback you need. "Is the order right?" gets better answers than "What do you think?".

Second, write down what was decided before the call ends. A short list of decisions and open questions stops the same topic from returning next week.

Third, keep the loop short. We aim for a week between drafts, and never more than two. A long gap lets assumptions grow unnoticed.

This rhythm asks for a little trust at the start, because early drafts look unfinished. Most teams find it calmer than the big reveal, because nobody is ever far from agreement.

## Begin with purpose

Most projects arrive with an answer already attached. A team asks for a new website, a dashboard or a set of templates. The request is sincere, but it hides the real question: what decision should this work make easier?

We spend the first days of every engagement finding that question. It is rarely the one in the brief. Once it is written down, it becomes a boundary for everything that follows.

## A worked example

Last spring a small museum asked us to redesign its events page. Attendance was falling, and the page felt dated. The obvious move was a fresh layout with bigger images.

We asked a different question first: why do people decide not to come? We read a year of visitor emails and sat with the front-desk team for two afternoons. The pattern was plain. People could not tell which events suited children, and they could not see prices before clicking through.

So the question became: how might a parent decide in under a minute whether an event is right for their family? That question changed the work. We added two plain labels to every event, age range and price, and moved them next to the title. The layout barely changed.

Bookings for family events rose over the next season. The museum kept its old design for another year, and nobody missed the redesign.

## Keep the question visible

A good question needs to stay in view, or it quietly slips away. We write ours at the top of every draft and read it aloud at the start of each review. When a new idea appears, we ask whether it helps answer the question. If it does not, it goes on a list for later.

This habit does not slow the work down. It removes the long debates about taste, because there is a shared test for every choice. It also makes endings clear: when the question is answered well enough, the work is done.

If you take one thing from this note, make it this. Before you choose a format, a feature or a tool, write one sentence that starts with "How might we help someone decide…". Then let that sentence judge everything else.

I take a lot of notes. For a long time almost none of them were ever read again. They went into apps, into notebooks, into files called *misc*, and they stayed there like letters posted to an address nobody checks. A few years ago I started asking a narrower question than "how should I take notes?" The question was: which of my notes are still useful a week later, and what do they have in common?

## Most notes are for the moment

The honest answer is that most notes should not survive the week, and that is fine. A phone number scribbled during a call, a list of points for a meeting, a sketch of an idea I am about to try: these do their work within hours. Keeping them forever does not make them more valuable; it just buries the few that matter under a growing pile.

So the first change was to let most notes die. I use a single pad of scrap paper for anything temporary, and the pad goes into the recycling every Friday without being reviewed. That one habit removed a surprising amount of guilt. The notes that belonged to the moment were allowed to stay there.

## What the survivors look like

The notes I still reach for after a week share a few traits.

**They make sense to a stranger.** The stranger is me, eight days later, without the context I had when I wrote them. "Fix the header thing" is useless by Thursday. "The header wraps below 400px because the logo has a fixed width" is still useful a year on.

**They hold one idea.** A note that contains a meeting summary, two unrelated ideas and a shopping list is hard to find and harder to use. A note that holds one idea can be moved, grouped, and combined with others.

**They say why.** A note that records a decision without its reason ages badly. The decision gets questioned, nobody remembers the reason, and the argument happens again. One extra sentence—*because the supplier only ships on Tuesdays*—saves the whole conversation.

**They are written, not captured.** Pasting a paragraph from somewhere else is capture. Restating it in my own words is writing. Only the second one tells me, later, that I understood it.

## Index cards

These traits pushed me toward a very old tool: the index card. A card is small enough that it can only hold one idea, and it is physical enough that writing on it feels deliberate. At the end of each week I go through the scrap pad and the week's meeting notes and ask whether anything deserves a card. Usually two or three things do. I write them out properly, in full sentences, with the reason included, and file them in a box by topic.

The box is not clever. There are no links, tags, or search. There are about a dozen dividers, and cards move between them when I reorganise my thinking. Pulling one card out of a stack and reading it in isolation is the fastest way I know to find out whether a note was written well.

## The weekly pass is the whole system

I have tried many note systems, and I now think the tool matters much less than the pass. Any system works if you regularly sit down, read what you wrote, and decide what to keep. No system works if you never do.

My pass takes about twenty minutes on a Friday, often in the [quiet hour](/articles/the-quiet-hour) before anyone else is around. Scrap pad in the recycling, meeting notes skimmed, two or three new cards written, one old card reread at random. That last step is my favourite. Sometimes the card is obsolete and goes in the bin. Sometimes it is exactly what I needed that week, and I have no idea how I forgot it.

The notes that survive the week are the ones I chose to keep, written for someone who was not there. That someone is always me.

Last year I read fewer books than in any year since university, and I remember more of them than I have in a decade. The change was not a new app or a reading challenge. I simply started reading slowly, on purpose, with a pencil in my hand and no target for how many pages to get through.

## Speed was hiding the problem

For years I read quickly because I thought quantity was the point. There were always more books on the list than time to read them, so I skimmed, skipped, and finished. At the end of the year I could list the titles. If you had asked me what any of them argued, I would have struggled.

Fast reading felt efficient because it produced a visible result: another book done. What it did not produce was understanding. The ideas passed through without catching on anything. I was treating reading like clearing an inbox, and getting about as much out of it.

## What slow reading looks like

Reading slowly does not mean reading every word at half speed. It means giving the book the kind of attention I would give a person I was talking to. In practice, a few habits:

- **One book at a time.** Juggling five meant none of them got my full attention. Now one sits on the table and the rest wait on the shelf.
- **A pencil, always.** I mark sentences that surprise me, write questions in the margin, and argue with the author when I disagree. The marks are for me, not for posterity.
- **Stop at the end of a chapter and say what it was about.** Out loud, or in two lines in the back of the book. If I cannot, I read the last few pages again.
- **Reread the good parts.** When a passage is dense or beautiful, I read it twice before moving on. This felt wasteful at first. It is now the part I look forward to.
- **Let myself stop.** A book that is not worth slow reading is not worth fast reading either. I put it down without guilt.

## The ribbon bookmark

My favourite small tool for this is the ribbon bookmark sewn into some hardback books. A loose bookmark falls out and gets lost; the ribbon stays. It sounds trivial, but it changed how I finish a reading session. Before closing the book, I lay the ribbon across the page at the exact line where I stopped. The next time I open it, I begin by rereading the paragraph before the ribbon, which drops me straight back into the author's line of thought.

That small ritual of ending and resuming turns a book into a long conversation rather than a series of disconnected sittings. It is the same instinct behind the [quiet hour](/articles/the-quiet-hour): give the attention a clear place to start, and it settles faster.

## Reading as craft

Slow reading turned out to be a skill, and like any craft it improves with practice. At first I was impatient, constantly checking how many pages were left. After a few months the impatience faded. I began noticing how writers built their arguments, where they were careful and where they were bluffing, which sentences did the real work. Those observations have fed back into my own writing more than any style guide.

It also changed what I choose to read. When each book costs more attention, I am pickier about which ones earn it. The list has become shorter and better. I reread old favourites more often, and find something new in them nearly every time.

## Try one chapter

You do not need to overhaul your reading life. Pick the book you are currently reading, take a pencil, and read the next chapter as slowly as it asks to be read. Mark what surprises you. When you reach the end, say in two lines what it was about. Then close the book and notice how much of it stays with you.

For me, that was enough to stop counting.

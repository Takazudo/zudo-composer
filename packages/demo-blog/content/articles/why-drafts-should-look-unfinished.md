When I share an early draft, I make it look early on purpose. Pencil instead of ink. Plain text instead of a laid-out document. Grey boxes where the pictures will go. It is not modesty and it is not laziness. How finished something looks changes the conversation people are willing to have about it, and in the early stages I want the rougher conversation.

## Polish invites the wrong kind of feedback

Show someone a beautifully typeset page and they will comment on the typesetting. They will notice that a heading is slightly too large, that two paragraphs have uneven spacing, that a word is misspelled in line four. All of that is real, and almost none of it matters yet. The questions I actually need answered—*is this the right argument? does the structure hold? is anything missing?*—get crowded out, because the page looks as if those questions were already settled.

A pencil sketch sends a different signal. It says the big decisions are still open. People feel free to say "what if the whole middle section went first?" because moving it costs nothing. The same suggestion made about a polished draft feels rude, and often goes unsaid.

## Finish is a promise

Every layer of finish implies a decision. Choosing a typeface implies the words are settled enough to be worth setting. Adding real photographs implies the layout is fixed. Tidy margins imply the content fits them. When I polish too early, I make those promises before I have earned them, and then I defend them longer than I should, because I have already invested the time.

There is a quieter cost too. Polishing is pleasant. It feels like progress and asks very little of the hard part of the mind. I have spent whole mornings adjusting the look of a draft whose second half did not yet make sense, and called it work. An unfinished-looking draft removes that temptation. There is nothing to polish, so the only thing left to do is think.

## Keeping the marks visible

The most useful drafts I keep are the messy ones: pages with lines struck through, arrows moving paragraphs, questions in the margin. I used to tidy those marks away before showing the draft to anyone. Now I leave them. The crossings-out show where I changed my mind, which is often where the interesting decisions were made. The margin questions tell a reader exactly where I am unsure, so they can spend their attention there instead of on the parts I am confident about.

A few habits help me keep drafts honest about their state:

1. **Match the finish to the question.** If I am asking about structure, I share an outline. If I am asking about tone, I share a few paragraphs. I only share a laid-out page when I am asking about the layout.
2. **Say what kind of feedback I want.** One line at the top—*looking for problems with the argument, not the wording*—saves everyone time.
3. **Leave placeholders obvious.** A grey box labelled *photo of the workshop* is better than a stock image that someone will mistake for a choice.

## When to switch

None of this means drafts should stay rough forever. There is a point where the big questions really are settled, and then finish becomes the work. Spacing, word choice and image selection deserve full attention once the argument is sound, and they deserve it from someone who is not still arguing with the structure.

The skill is noticing the moment of the switch. My test is simple: when I stop moving paragraphs around, the draft has earned some polish. Until then, it should look exactly as unfinished as it is. The same idea runs underneath Mina's piece on [the half-finished list](/articles/the-half-finished-list): showing the true state of the work is kinder, to other people and to yourself, than pretending it is further along.

I get up an hour before I need to. Not to exercise, not to answer email early, not to squeeze more work into the day. The hour has no task attached to it at all. I make tea, sit in the chair by the kitchen window, and let my attention settle before anyone else gets a claim on it.

It is the most productive hour of my day, and I do almost nothing in it.

## Attention has a morning state

For most of the day my attention is spoken for. Messages arrive, meetings start, other people's priorities fill the gaps between my own. By mid-morning I am responding more than choosing. That is not a complaint; responding is a large part of any job. But it means the hours when I could think freely are few, and they are almost always at the edges of the day.

Early morning is the widest of those edges. Nothing has happened yet. There is no thread to follow, no reply I am waiting for. The mind, left alone, starts to wander toward whatever it has been quietly working on. Often that turns out to be the problem I could not solve yesterday.

## What I do with the hour

Very little, by design. The rules are these:

- **No screens.** The phone stays in another room until the hour ends. This is the only rule that is not negotiable.
- **No agenda.** I do not plan to think about anything in particular. If a problem shows up, I let it. If nothing does, I look out of the window.
- **Something to write with.** A notebook and pencil sit on the sill. I rarely write more than a few lines, but when a thought arrives I want to catch it before the day washes it away.
- **The same place.** The chair, the window, the cup. Doing it in the same spot every day makes it easy to slip into without deciding to.

Some mornings I read a few pages of a book. Some mornings I just watch the light change on the wall opposite. Both count.

## Why it pays off

The hour works because it gives my attention a chance to find its own level before the day pulls it somewhere else. Ideas that need slow thinking—how to structure a long piece, what to say in a difficult conversation, whether a project is worth continuing—rarely come to me while I am busy. They come when there is room for them.

It also changes the rest of the day. Starting from quiet, I am more likely to choose the first real task deliberately instead of reaching for whatever is loudest. The [one thing I put on my desk](/articles/a-desk-with-one-thing-on-it) at eight o'clock is usually something I decided on in the chair at seven.

## Protecting it

The hour is easy to lose. One early meeting, one late night, one morning of checking the phone "just for a second," and the habit starts to slip. I protect it the way I would protect an appointment with someone else. If the hour has to move, it moves; it does not disappear.

I also try not to make it into another task. The moment the quiet hour becomes a place to *get things done*—clear the inbox, plan the week, study—it stops working. Its value is precisely that nothing is expected of it.

You do not need a full hour, and you do not need the morning. Twenty minutes at the end of the day works for some people. The only requirements are that nobody else can claim the time, and that you do not claim it for anything either. Sit somewhere comfortable, put the screen away, and see where your attention goes when you stop telling it where to go.

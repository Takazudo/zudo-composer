The first thing I was taught at a woodworking bench was not how to cut. It was how to sharpen. My teacher put a dull chisel in my hand, a whetstone in front of me, and a jar of water beside it, and said we would start the real work when the edge could shave the hair on my forearm. That took most of an afternoon.

I resented it at the time. I had come to make a box. Years later it is the lesson I use most, and hardly ever at a bench.

## A dull edge hides its cost

A dull chisel still cuts. That is the problem. It crushes the wood fibres instead of slicing them, so every stroke needs more force, and more force means less control. The cut wanders. You push harder to correct it, and the edge slips. Nothing about the tool announces that it is the cause; it simply feels as if the work is difficult and you are not very good at it.

Most of the tools I use now are not steel. They are a text editor, a notebook, a calendar, a way of naming files, a small set of habits for starting a day. Each of them can go dull in exactly the same quiet way. A shortcut I never learned. A folder structure that made sense three projects ago. A template full of sections nobody fills in. The work still gets done, with a little more force than it should need, and I blame the work.

## Sharpening is a separate act

The discipline my teacher was really teaching was the separation. You do not sharpen in the middle of a cut. You stop, put the workpiece down, and give the edge your full attention for ten minutes. Then you go back to cutting and give that your full attention too.

I try to keep the same boundary with non-steel tools. When I notice that something is fighting me, I write it on a card instead of fixing it on the spot. Fixing it immediately feels efficient but breaks two kinds of focus at once: the task I was doing, and the repair, which deserves better than a distracted five minutes. On Friday afternoons I take the cards out and sharpen. I rename the folders. I delete the template sections. I learn the one shortcut I have reached for twenty times this week.

## How to tell when an edge is ready

At the bench, the test is simple and physical: the edge catches on a fingernail, or it does not. With other tools I use a rougher test. After sharpening, the next use should feel almost boring. If I still have to think about the tool while doing the task, it is not sharp yet; if I only think about the task, it is.

A few questions help me find the dull edges:

1. What do I do every day that takes more than three steps?
2. What do I avoid starting because the setup is annoying?
3. Where do I keep correcting the same small mistake?

The answers are rarely dramatic. They are almost always cheap to fix, which is exactly why they go unfixed for so long.

## Not an excuse to stop working

There is a trap here, and woodworkers know it well: the person who sharpens forever and never cuts. A perfect edge is satisfying in a way that finished work often is not, because it asks nothing of your judgement. I have lost weeks to rebuilding a note-taking system instead of taking notes.

My teacher's rule protects against that as well. You sharpen until the edge passes the test, and not a stroke more. Then you cut. The point of a sharp tool is that it disappears in your hand and leaves your attention for the wood.

> Sharpen before you cut, and stop sharpening when it cuts.

That is still the most useful sentence anyone has said to me about work. It pairs well with Teodor's later note on [choosing tools you can repair](/articles/choosing-tools-you-can-repair), because an edge you can maintain yourself is one you will actually keep sharp.

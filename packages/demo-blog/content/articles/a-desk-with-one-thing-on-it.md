For about a month I kept only one thing on my desk at a time. Not one kind of thing, one object: a notebook, or a laptop, or a book, or a single printed page. Everything else lived on a shelf across the room. It started as an experiment after a bad week and became the way I work.

## What a crowded desk asks of you

I used to think of my desk as neutral. Objects sat on it; I ignored the ones I was not using. It turns out I was not ignoring them at all. Each one was a small unfinished sentence. The unopened letter said *deal with me*. The stack of books said *you were going to read these*. The second notebook said *you started something else, remember?*

None of these requests was loud. Together they made a steady background hum that I only noticed when it stopped. The first morning with a bare desk felt strangely quiet, the way a room does when a fridge you had stopped hearing switches off.

## The rules I settled on

The rules were simple, and I broke them often enough to learn why they mattered.

- **One object on the desk.** A pen counts as part of a notebook; a cup of tea does not count at all.
- **To change objects, walk.** Putting the notebook back on the shelf and fetching the laptop takes perhaps twenty seconds. That short walk turned out to be the point.
- **Nothing is stored on the desk overnight.** The desk starts each morning empty, and the first decision of the day is what to put on it.

The walk was the part I resisted and the part that changed the most. Switching tasks at a crowded desk costs nothing, so I did it constantly, sometimes every few minutes. When switching meant standing up and crossing the room, I noticed each switch. Often I noticed it before I stood up, and sat back down.

## What changed

The obvious result was fewer interruptions of my own making. I had assumed most of my distractions came from outside: messages, noise, other people. A good share of them came from the objects in my peripheral vision, each quietly offering an easier task than the one in front of me.

The less obvious result was that choosing became visible. With one object on the desk, the question *what am I doing right now?* always has a physical answer. If the answer is the laptop, I know I chose the laptop. If I find myself reading the news on it, I can see the gap between the choice and the behaviour, and that gap is easier to close when it is not hidden among six other things.

It also made the ends of tasks cleaner. Putting the notebook back on the shelf is a small ceremony that says *this is done for now*. I had never had that before. Work used to trail off rather than stop.

## What it is not

A bare desk is not a moral position, and I do not think clutter is a character flaw. Some work genuinely needs several things at once: a reference open beside a draft, a sketch next to a model. When that happens I clear enough space for the task and nothing more, and I put it all away when I stop.

It is also not about the desk. The desk was just the easiest place to see a pattern I had everywhere: on my screen, in my calendar, in the [list I carry through the day](/articles/the-half-finished-list). One thing in front of me, chosen on purpose, everything else a short walk away. I keep coming back to that shape.

If you try it, give it a week. The first two days are uncomfortable. By the fifth, a crowded desk starts to look like a room full of people all talking at once.
